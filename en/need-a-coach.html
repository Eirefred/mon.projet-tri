Why a triathlon coach?

For triathletes asking the question: the value of a coach, and what training involves for your goal.

The short answer: it all depends on your goal. To get fit, no coach needed. To improve, aim further, or if your body needs special attention, a good coach makes a real difference.

Coach or no coach? The debate comes up often in triathlon: "I learned everything on my own, and I finished my Ironman!" That's a great achievement. But every athlete responds differently to training, and a poorly adapted plan can slow progress or lead to injury.

🏃Just want to get fit? No coach needed

  • For your health and well-being, you don't need a coach.
  • Benchmark: 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity per week[1].
Read moreShow less

The World Health Organization recommends that adults get 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity per week, plus muscle strengthening twice a week[1][2]. Swimming, cycling and running are a great way to get there.

To get started, a fitness program, a club or a first short triathlon is enough. Consistency matters far more than the perfect program. Our Self-Guided toolbox is there for that.

🎯Want to improve or take on a challenge? A coach makes the difference

  • Longer distance, faster times, an injury or a specific condition: a coach makes the difference.
  • Training alone with a free generic plan found online risks stagnation, regression or injury[5].
Read moreShow less

As soon as you aim for a longer distance, faster times or an unusual challenge, or you're coming back from an injury or living with a specific condition, a coach makes the difference. Triathlon also means balancing three sports together: bike fatigue shows up in the run, and swim technique matters as much as fitness.

Guidance improves results: on the same program, supervised athletes gained 33% in strength versus 25% unsupervised[3]. A coach is also a safeguard against overtraining[4].

A generic plan found online, on the other hand, is written for an "average" athlete who doesn't exist. It can be too easy (you stagnate), too hard (you regress) or too fast: when load jumps from one week to the next, injury risk more than doubles[5].

📈Progress gradually, and adapt to life

  • Benchmark: increase your training load (volume or intensity) by no more than about 10% from one week to the next[5].
  • A plan is never set in stone: a good coach is attentive and ready to adapt it.
Read moreShow less

The body adapts to a load that rises a little, not to one that jumps. A common benchmark: increase your training load (volume or intensity) by no more than about 10% from one week to the next[5]. It's not a magic rule[17][18]: the right pace depends on your starting point and recovery. You don't increase everything at once, and you respect loads and rest periods when needed. Depending on the chosen strategy, training can be organized in cycles or continuously: it's up to the coach to choose what suits you.

Above all, a plan is never set in stone. A busy work week, a sick child, a bad night, a new pain, a heat wave: the plan must be adjusted regularly. A competent coach is attentive to your feedback and willing to adapt your training as often as needed.

🧩Every athlete is unique

  • On an identical program, gains vary enormously from one person to another[6].
  • Hormones, body type, history and daily life: a good plan accounts for them.
Read moreShow less

Two people following the same program don't get the same benefits: in the HERITAGE study, nearly half of the difference in gains is explained by genetics[6]. The rest depends on sleep, stress, nutrition and how training is dosed[7].

🧬 Hormonal profileage, sex, cycle, diabetes…

Recovery slows with age[8]. The effect of the menstrual cycle varies from one woman to another[9]. Diabetes requires adjusting insulin and carbs[10]. Eating too little disrupts hormones[11].

📏 Body typesex, weight, height

Men and women don't fatigue the same way[12]. Weight affects running impact and buoyancy.

📖 Historyoriginal sport, injuries

Starting from the couch or with a swimming background means neither the same starting point nor the same weak spots. A past injury also calls for adapted progression.

🏠 Daily lifework, family, sleep

Time available for training is limited by work and family life: the plan has to fit around it. And life stress adds to training stress, which raises injury risk[13].

  • An athlete who succeeded training alone isn't necessarily a good coach for others[14].
  • The relationship rests on trust and a shared vision[15].
  • The athlete does their part: prescribed sessions completed, honesty, transparency and ethics.

A good coach

An athlete who trained alone and reached their goals has every reason to be proud. But that doesn't necessarily make them a good coach: what worked for them, with their body, history and life, won't necessarily work for someone else. Coaching means adapting.

A good coach combines knowledge (anatomy, physiology, planning, nutrition), people skills (listening, empathy, psychology) and a drive to keep learning[14], all guided by ethics centred on the athlete.

You don't need to be a kinesiologist: a bachelor's degree is a good foundation, but triathlon expertise also comes from specialization, experience and recognized certifications, such as those of the National Coaching Certification Program (NCCP) in triathlon. A coach can be trained or certified through Triathlon Québec (in French) or Triathlon Canada.

A responsible coach has also completed ethics training (for example the NCCP "Making Ethical Decisions" module) and has undergone a criminal background check. These are two pillars of the Coaching Association of Canada's Responsible Coaching Movement. Don't hesitate to ask.

A good match

Even the best coach isn't right for everyone. A good relationship rests on trust, commitment, complementarity and a shared vision of the goals[15][16]. That's why at mon Projet Tri, we take the time to get to know you before matching you with an NCCP-certified coach or one recognized by Triathlon Québec.

A committed athlete

A good coach can't do it all alone: the athlete has to do their part too. Together, coach and athlete form a team that is stronger than the sum of its parts. In practice, that means:

  • Complete the prescribed sessions as planned: doing more or less without saying so skews the reading of your progress.
  • Be honest and transparent: missed session, pain, fatigue, stress, schedule change. That information is what allows the plan to be adjusted.
  • Act ethically: respect the rules and the spirit of sport (fair play, no doping), as well as your coach's time and commitment.

💸Is a coach expensive?

  • Personalized coaching is above all human time: a good coach spends hours personalizing your plan, then adjusting it.
  • A price that's too low doesn't let a coach give you that time.
  • A fair price means a coach who is available and committed to you.
Read moreShow less

A good coach usually spends hours personalizing your training plan and your season planning. And every week, there's more work: reading your feedback, analyzing your sessions, adjusting the plan, answering your questions, and continuing to learn. Taking care of you takes time.

The cheaper the coaching, the less time a coach can give you while making a living from it. They then have to take on more and more athletes, or send everyone the same plan.

Conversely, for a coach to give you their time and knowledge, it has to be viable for them. A fair price is what allows them to be attentive, to respond and to adjust your plan week after week.

A high price doesn't guarantee quality either: rely on the list below to judge good coaching.

✅ A good coach is easy to recognize: make sure yours…

  1. asks you questions and takes the time to get to know you before starting: goals, sports and injury history, availability, daily life;
  2. builds a plan for you, not a copy-paste plan;
  3. increases load gradually and plans the rest you need, depending on the chosen strategy (in cycles or continuously);
  4. keeps asking you questions throughout (how you feel, sleep, fatigue, pain) and adjusts the plan based on your answers;
  5. adapts the plan when life changes (work, illness, vacation), without making you "catch up" all at once;
  6. explains the why behind your sessions;
  7. is trained and screened (NCCP, ethics training, criminal background check) and knows their limits: refers you to a health professional when needed.

⚠️Warning signs: the same plan for everyone · sudden jumps in load · pain or illness ignored ("just push through").

If several of these points are missing, talk about it with your coach: a good relationship relies on transparency, both ways.

Volume, number of sessions, type of effort: training changes a lot depending on your goal. And progress has to adjust to your life.

FAQ: coach or no coach?

Coach or no coach: do I need a triathlon coach?

It all depends on your goal. To get moving and stay fit, you don't need a coach. To improve, aim for a longer distance or a faster time, or if your body needs special attention, a good coach makes a real difference.

Can I train for a triathlon on my own with a free plan?

Yes, to get fit or do a first short triathlon. But a generic plan is written for an "average" athlete: it can be too easy, too hard or increase load too fast, which risks stagnation, regression or injury. Nor does it adjust to your life (work, illness, vacation).

Is a triathlon coach worth the cost?

Personalized coaching is human time: a good coach spends hours personalizing your plan, then adjusting it based on your feedback. A price that's too low doesn't let them give you that time; a fair price lets the coach be available and committed. A high price doesn't guarantee quality either.

Do I need a coach to do a triathlon?

Not necessarily. To get fit or do a first short triathlon, a fitness program, a club or a toolbox is enough. To improve, move up in distance, take on a challenge like a half-Ironman or an Ironman, or if you have injuries or a specific condition, a coach makes a real difference.

How many hours per week should I train for a triathlon?

As an example only: about 3 hours per week for a try-a-tri, 8 hours to be competitive in sprint or Olympic, 10 hours for a half-Ironman, 14 hours for an Ironman and 20 hours for Ironman performance. Your actual week depends on your profile and should be adapted by a coach.

How much can I increase my training load?

A common benchmark is to increase your training load (volume or intensity) by no more than about 10% from one week to the next, while respecting the rest you need. It's not a magic rule: the right pace depends on your starting point and recovery.

Do you need to be a kinesiologist to be a good triathlon coach?

No. A kinesiology degree is a good foundation, but triathlon expertise also comes from specialization, experience and recognized certifications such as the triathlon NCCP, along with ethics training and a criminal background check.

How do I recognize a good triathlon coach?

A good coach asks you questions, builds a plan for you, increases load gradually, adjusts the plan to your feedback and your life, explains the why behind your sessions, is trained and screened, and knows their limits.
